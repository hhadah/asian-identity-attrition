    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
            \begin{quotation}
                \textbf{R: } 3. I can't understand the bootstrap at the end of section 5. It is trivial to calculate the derivative or differential for each individual with respect to a change in anti-Asian bias. The explanation does not make it clear which is being calculated, and, if the latter, the magnitude of the change. Normally, we would report the mean rather than the median, although there is nothing fundamentally wrong with a median. My interpretation is that the author used the bootstrap to calculate the confidence interval of the estimated median derivative/differential, but this should be clarified.
            \end{quotation}
    \end{spacing}
    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
        \textbf{Response:} The reported statistic is the median across bootstrap replications of the sample-average derivative, not the median derivative across individuals. The previous description was unclear and the figure captions were wrong: they said the marginal effects held other variables ``at their sample means,'' whereas the code evaluates them at each individual's own covariate values and averages over the sample. The paragraph now reads:
        \begin{quote}\textcolor{blue}{Because multinomial logit coefficients are log-odds ratios relative to the ``Asian only'' category, I report average marginal effects on the probability of each identity. For anti-Asian bias, the marginal effect for each child is the derivative of the predicted probability of an outcome with respect to the bias index, evaluated at that child's own covariate values; I average these derivatives, without weights, over the estimation sample. The reported number is therefore an average slope expressed per index unit. Because one unit is large relative to the observed variation in bias (Section~\ref{sub:lw-bias}), it should not be read as the exact change in probability that a one-unit shift in bias would produce.\footnote{The derivative is computed numerically as a central difference with a step of one-tenth of the standard deviation of the index across children in the estimation sample, not one-tenth of an index unit; the perturbation is truncated at the sample range of the index, which pulls the derivatives for children in the most and least biased cells slightly toward zero.} For the binary covariates (female, mother college graduate, father college graduate), the marginal effect is the average over the sample of the change in predicted probability from switching the indicator from 0 to 1. For inference, I draw 1{,}000 bootstrap samples of children with replacement, stratified by region $\times$ year cell, re-estimate the model in each, and recompute the sample-average marginal effects. The point estimates I report are the medians of these 1{,}000 sample averages, not medians across children, and the confidence intervals are their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Because the bootstrap resamples children rather than states, these intervals reflect sampling variation in the individual data and do not account for dependence among children in the same state. I therefore use the state-clustered pooled model of Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pooled-mnl-bias-x-aw} for inference about the pooled bias effect and the AW--WA contrast, and report it alongside the bootstrap estimates.}\end{quote}
        A footnote adds that the derivative is computed numerically as a central difference with a step of one-tenth of the sample standard deviation of the index, truncated at the sample range. The step is not necessarily 0.1 index units. The paragraph also states what the bootstrap does and does not capture: it resamples children, stratified by region $\times$ year cell, and does not account for dependence within states. I therefore use the state-clustered pooled model in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pooled-mnl-bias-x-aw} for inference about the pooled bias effect and the AW--WA contrast. Its common-effect average marginal effect on ``White only'' identification is $+$14.6 percentage points per index unit, with a state-clustered standard error of 5.7. The table also compares conventional and state-clustered standard errors, but that comparison does not measure how much the bootstrap understates uncertainty. All figure captions that describe these estimates (Figure~\ref{fig:marginal-effects-pooled} and Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:marginal-effects-second-parental}, \ref{fig:marginal-effects-second-adults-pooled}, and~\ref{fig:marginal-effects-third-grandparental}) were corrected to match. I retain the median summaries reported in the previous submission and state this choice explicitly. The median is the 50th percentile of the bootstrap distribution, not necessarily the midpoint of its percentile confidence interval.
    \end{spacing}

    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
            \begin{quotation}
                \textbf{R: } 4. I also found the first full paragraph on page 17 somewhat confusing. I infer that the author estimated the model separately for AW and WA individuals. If the question is ``are the coefficients the same for the two groups,'' the proper test is a likelihood ratio test. Comparing the coefficients is problematic since most MNL programs normalize the variance of the error terms. Comparing the median derivative is problematic because the derivative depends on the value of the other controls. If the question is whether bias affects AW and WA individuals differently, it probably makes most sense to assume the rest of the model is the same for the two groups and just interact bias with AW (or WA).
            \end{quotation}
    \end{spacing}
    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
        \textbf{Response:} I estimated the model the referee describes and report it as Appendix Table~\ref{tab:pooled-mnl-bias-x-aw}: a single multinomial logit on the pooled AW and WA children, with every coefficient constrained to be equal across the two groups except an interaction between bias and an AW indicator, with the same controls, weights, and region $\times$ year dummies as the main models. Because the multinomial logit routine does not provide clustered standard errors, I built a state-clustered sandwich covariance from the weighted scores and the information matrix. The script checks this calculation against a standard clustered covariance on a two-category fit, where a multinomial logit is a binary logit, using a relative tolerance of 0.1 percent. The table reports the coefficients with clustered standard errors, a Wald test of the two interaction terms using that covariance, and the likelihood-ratio test the referee mentions, labelled as assuming independent observations. It also reports the average marginal effect of bias for AW and for WA families evaluated over the same pooled sample, which addresses the referee's point that derivatives depend on the other controls. The bias $\times$ AW interaction in the ``White only'' equation is 0.89 (standard error 0.32); the clustered Wald test rejects equal bias effects ($\chi^2(2) = 10.5$, $p = 0.005$), as does the likelihood-ratio test. With the covariate distribution held fixed, the average marginal effect on ``White only'' identification per bias-index unit is 24.9 percentage points in AW families and 9.9 in WA families, a difference of 15.1 points (standard error 4.2). The corresponding difference for ``Asian only'' is $-10.5$ points (standard error 6.4, $p = 0.10$), and the ``Asian and White'' difference is not distinguishable from zero. A footnote also reports the analogous linear probability model for the binary Asian indicator, whose bias $\times$ AW interaction is $-0.08$ (standard error 0.06). This linear-probability-model estimate is not statistically distinguishable from zero; its sign agrees with the multinomial result, but it is not independent confirmation of a difference. The Results paragraph now explains why comparisons across separately estimated models require care, presents the pooled test alongside the between-model tests, and states the conclusion in terms of the pooled estimates. The Conclusion quotes both numbers.
    \end{spacing}

        \textbf{Response:} I added this discussion to the robustness section, with the referee's interpretation. After re-clustering (see comment 6), neither column is statistically distinguishable from zero, so the discussion is framed around the point estimates:
        \begin{quote}\textcolor{blue}{The opposite signs for Asian-born mothers and fathers deserve comment, because in a closed marriage market every Asian woman who marries a White man is matched by an Asian man who does not marry an Asian woman. This sample is not a closed market. It conditions on a co-resident child and at least one Asian-born parent, and it includes both Asian--Asian and mixed-parentage couples. Among mixed-parentage families, an Asian-born mother is far more common than an Asian-born father: in the descriptive sample in Table~\ref{tab:asianbygen}, 20,247 children have a White father and an Asian mother, compared with 9,055 with an Asian father and a White mother. A bias-induced change in whether Asian men marry at all, or in whether they form a family with a co-resident child, would appear as a difference between the two columns rather than as an interracial marriage. The estimates are therefore consistent with bias affecting marriage itself for Asian men, not only partner choice, although their imprecision means the data do not settle this.}\end{quote}
    \end{spacing}

    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
            \begin{quotation}
                \textbf{R: } 6. The footnote to Table 7 says that standard errors are clustered at the household level. I assume this is just a version-control error and that the errors are clustered at the state level as in the other similar tables. Incidentally, the footnotes in some of the tables, including 7, are not numbered consecutively.
            \end{quotation}
    \end{spacing}
    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
        \textbf{Response:} It was not a version-control error; the regressions behind Table 7 were in fact clustered on the household. The referee is right that this is the wrong level for a regressor that varies by state and year, so I re-estimated the table with state-clustered standard errors. The point estimates are unchanged; the standard errors on bias rise from 0.01 to 0.03, and the bias coefficients (0.04, $-0.01$, and 0.03) are no longer statistically distinguishable from zero. The text now reports them as such and a footnote records the change. Footnotes in Tables~\ref{regtab-mig-01} and~\ref{regtab-logit-02} and in Appendix Table~\ref{regtab-logit-03} are now numbered consecutively. Appendix Table~\ref{regtab-logit-03} also corrects the previous mismatch between displayed log-odds and a note describing odds ratios; that separate correction is disclosed under Other changes.
    \end{spacing}

    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
            \begin{quotation}
                \textbf{R: } 7. This is the one change I would absolutely insist on if I were the editor. The results are not robust to state fixed effects. The base model has region/year fixed effects. When you add fixed state effects to that model, the results are too imprecise to be informative, as I would expect. You fail to get a significant effect even if you limit the model to state and year effects. So, this does leave open the possibility that there is something different about states with high anti-Asian bias that also leads to lower Asian identity among individuals of mixed Asian/other ancestry. Since I do not have a compelling missing variable that fits this description, I do not believe that it detracts heavily from the paper. Still, you should recognize it as a limitation of your study, not make it sound as if your results are robust to including state fixed effects.
            \end{quotation}
    \end{spacing}
    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
        \textbf{Response:} I agree and have made this change; my response to the editor's second point quotes the new paragraph. Appendix Table~\ref{regtab-all-fe} reports an all-generation linear probability model, including children with two Asian-born parents and generation controls, rather than a state-fixed-effects version of the headline mixed-race multinomial logit. Within that linear probability model, adding state effects lowers the bias estimate and leaves it statistically indistinguishable from zero. The precise within-state estimate comes only from a specification with no time controls. The possibility the referee describes cannot be ruled out, and the limitation is repeated in the Conclusion.
    \end{spacing}

    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
            \begin{quotation}
                \textbf{R: } 8. Figures 3 and 4 are never discussed, just mentioned. They should be dropped. Similarly, I don't see what I learn from Figures 5--8 that is not in Tables 4 and 5. I leave it to the editor as to whether Figures 9--13 could be presented more concisely and effectively in tabular form. I feel confident that Figures 14--16 would work better as a single table.
            \end{quotation}
    \end{spacing}
    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
        \textbf{Response:} Done as the editor directed: Figures 3--8, 10, 11, and 13 are moved to the appendix, Figures 9 and 12 are combined into a single two-panel figure, and Figures 14--16 are replaced by Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity-interactions}. My response to the editor's first point lists the new locations.
    \end{spacing}

    \subsection*{Other changes}
    \begin{spacing}{\rrxspc}
        I also made the following corrections. First, the captions of the marginal-effects figures previously said that other covariates were held ``at their sample means''; the estimates are averages over individuals, and the captions and Section~\ref{sec:empstrat} now say so (see comment 3). Second, the notes to the new Table~\ref{tab:heterogeneity-interactions} describe the controls the interaction regressions include; the old figure captions listed two controls the regressions do not include (see the editor's first point). Third, one sentence in the Conclusion stated that studies relying on self-reported race ``may overstate Asian American socioeconomic success.'' Under negative selection on maternal education, measured outcomes instead understate the outcomes of people of Asian ancestry in high-bias areas. The revised discussion states this implication conditionally and identifies Asian-father/White-mother families as those supporting the maternal-college interaction. Fourth, Section~\ref{sub:lw-bias} defines the unit of the bias index and reports its between-state and within-state standard deviations (see comment 1); the paper now distinguishes one index unit from one empirical standard deviation. Fifth, the migration paragraph in Section~\ref{sec:robcheck} quoted a 0.06 standard deviation difference in bias between movers' current and birth states; the coefficient in Table~\ref{regtab-mig-01}, column (3), is 0.02 index units (standard error 0.04), and the text now matches the table. Sixth, the sample descriptions for the mixed-parentage groups now say that the non-Asian parent was born outside Asia (86 percent of them in the United States), which is how the samples are constructed; the AW and WA labels are retained as the paper's convention. Seventh, because the bootstrap behind the figures and the between-model tests resamples children rather than states, the text labels differences supported only by that bootstrap as suggestive. State-clustered inference is reported separately for the pooled bias effect and the AW--WA bias contrast; the difference between conventional and clustered standard errors does not quantify the bootstrap's understatement of uncertainty. Eighth, Appendix Table~\ref{regtab-logit-03} previously displayed log-odds while its note called them exponentiated coefficients. The table now displays odds ratios, so its coefficient changes include a scale correction, not just the change in standard-error clustering. The replication code adds four scripts that build the new tables and the variance decomposition.

        I also corrected the title, caption, and discussion of Figure~\ref{fig:toptwobias} to make clear that its two panels display different sets of places, rather than a matched-state comparison. The caption now lists the places in each panel; the plotted data are unchanged.
    \end{spacing}
